## Overview

This chapter goes deep on **gross income**: what actually triggers it (realization vs. recognition), when it counts as "received" for tax purposes, who has to report it when income could arguably belong to more than one person, and how the rules shift in community property states. It then runs through the main categories of income (services, property, annuities, asset sales, and a long list of "other" income sources like alimony, prizes, Social Security, and imputed income), and closes with the major **exclusion provisions** — the specific carve-outs that let taxpayers permanently keep certain income out of the tax base.

## Realization and Recognition of Income

### What Is Included in Gross Income?

§61(a) defines gross income about as broadly as possible: all income from any source, unless the code says otherwise somewhere else. The section lists examples — compensation, business income, rents, royalties, interest, dividends — but that list isn't exhaustive; it's illustrative of a catch-all rule. The Treasury regulations reinforce this: income counts whether it shows up as cash, property, or services, unless a specific exclusion applies.

Putting the statute, the regs, and case law together, gross income gets recognized when three things are all true:
- The taxpayer receives an actual economic benefit.
- That benefit is *realized* (see below).
- No provision lets them exclude or defer it.

#### Economic Benefit

You need to actually receive something of value for gross income to exist. Typical categories: compensation (cash, property, or services received for work), proceeds from selling property (cash, property, or debt relief), and income from investments or business activity (business income, rents, interest, dividends).

Borrowing money is the classic non-example: even though cash comes in, it's fully offset by the obligation to repay it, so no net economic benefit occurs — no gross income.

#### Realization Principle

Income is "realized" when (1) the taxpayer transacts with another party, and (2) that transaction produces a measurable change in property rights — something of determinable value changes hands.

Two reasons this realization-based approach makes sense as a rule:
- It lets income be measured objectively, since both parties to the deal have to agree on value.
- When cash is involved, the transaction itself typically hands the taxpayer the means to pay the resulting tax — so they're less likely to need to sell something else just to cover the bill. (Note: realization still occurs even without a cash cushion, e.g., when property or services are received instead of cash.)

#### Recognition

Once income is realized, it generally must be *recognized* — reported as gross income — unless a specific provision excuses it. This is the key distinction: **exclusions** permanently keep certain realized income out of the tax base, while **deferrals** just push recognition to a future year. Realized ≠ automatically recognized.

### Other Income Concepts

#### Form of Receipt

Cash isn't required for income to count — property or services work just as well. Barter transactions are a common trap: people trading goods/services through barter arrangements often (wrongly) assume no tax consequence, but the fair market value of what's exchanged is still taxable income, cash or not. The obligation to report realized income exists regardless of form, and regardless of whether the IRS is likely to find out.

#### Return of Capital Principle

When you sell property, you're allowed to recover your cost basis tax-free before anything counts as gain. So gain = sale proceeds minus unrecovered basis. If proceeds fall short of basis, that shortfall is a loss (only deductible if the code specifically allows it) rather than negative income. When proceeds come in over multiple periods (installment-style), the law generally spreads the capital recovery proportionally across each payment rather than front- or back-loading it.

#### Recovery of Amounts Previously Deducted

A refund is usually not taxable — it's just capital coming back, or an expense reversal. But if the refunded item was *deducted* in an earlier year, the **tax benefit rule** kicks in: the refund becomes taxable, but only to the extent the earlier deduction actually reduced taxes. This gets messier for itemizers, since (a) itemized deductions are subject to various caps that might have limited the original benefit, and (b) a deduction only helped at all if total itemized deductions exceeded the standard deduction that year.

### When Do Taxpayers Recognize Income?

Individuals typically report on a calendar-year basis; corporations often use a fiscal year. Either way, the taxpayer's accounting method decides which year realized income actually gets recognized.

#### Accounting Methods

- **Accrual method** (mandatory for most large corporations) — income recognized when earned, expenses deducted when the liability is incurred, regardless of cash timing.
- **Cash method** (most individuals) — income recognized when actually received (cash, property, or services), expenses deducted when actually paid, regardless of when earned/incurred.

Cash method advantages: it's far simpler for the average person to apply, and it gives taxpayers more control over *when* income lands and expenses go out — which makes timing-based tax planning easier.

#### Constructive Receipt

Cash-method taxpayers sometimes try to push income into next year by literally not cashing a check or not collecting a payment until after year-end. Courts shut this down with the **constructive receipt doctrine**: income is recognized once it's constructively received, meaning it's been credited to the taxpayer's account, or it's unconditionally available with no restrictions and the taxpayer knows it's there — even if they haven't physically collected it.

#### Claim of Right

Addresses the opposite timing wrinkle: income received in one year that might need to be paid back later. Under the **claim of right doctrine**, if a taxpayer receives income with no restrictions on using it (no obligation to repay), it's realized then — even if there's some chance it gets clawed back in a future year.

#### Who Recognizes the Income?

This becomes relevant when someone tries to shift income to a related, lower-taxed person — e.g., a high-bracket parent trying to redirect a paycheck to a low-bracket child.

##### Assignment of Income

Courts built the **assignment of income doctrine** specifically to block this. The rule: income from services is taxed to whoever performed the services, full stop — you can't just reassign it. Income from property (interest, dividends) is taxed to whoever *owns* the property while the income accrues. So the only way to genuinely shift income from property is to actually transfer ownership of the underlying asset, not just redirect the payment.

#### Community Property Systems

Most states use common law property rules, but nine use **community property** systems: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. Under community property, income from either spouse's labor is treated as earned equally by both, and property acquired during the marriage is generally owned equally (property brought into the marriage stays separate).

**Community property state consequences (federal tax):**
- Half of one spouse's service income is attributed to the other spouse.
- Half of community property income is attributed to each spouse.
- In AZ, CA, NV, NM, and WA: income from *separately* owned property stays entirely with the owning spouse.
- In ID, LA, TX, and WI: income from separately owned property is still split 50/50 between spouses.

**Common law state consequences (federal tax):**
- All service income stays with the spouse who earned it.
- Income from separately-owned property stays entirely with the owner.
- Income from jointly-owned property is split according to each co-owner's ownership share (e.g., 50/50 for two joint owners, proportional for three or more).

If a couple files jointly, none of this matters — both incomes get combined either way. It only matters for couples filing separately, where the outcome depends on whether they're in a common law state, a community property state that splits separate-property income, or one that doesn't.

## Types of Income

### Income From Services

Labor income — salary, wages, fees for nonemployee work, even unemployment compensation — is one of the most common (and rarely exempt) categories. Often called **earned income**, since it's generated directly by the taxpayer's own effort (this includes business income too, even from a business that sells inventory).

### Income From Property

Often called **unearned income**: gains/losses on property sales, dividends, interest, rents, royalties, annuities. Tax treatment varies by type — qualified dividends and long-term capital gains get preferential rates, while most other unearned income is taxed at ordinary rates. Some gains/losses get recognized immediately; others get deferred indefinitely depending on the type of transaction.

### Annuities

An annuity pays out a fixed stream of payments over time — commonly used for retirement income. Two flavors: fixed-term annuities, and life annuities (paid for as long as the person lives).

The tax challenge: each payment is part taxable income, part tax-free return of the original investment. The split is determined by the **annuity exclusion ratio**:

```
Annuity Exclusion Ratio = Original Investment / Expected Value of Annuity = Return of Capital Percentage
```

For fixed-term annuities, this is simple — original investment divided by number of payments. For life annuities, the "expected value" depends on life expectancy, which the IRS provides via actuarial tables (e.g., a 70-year-old at the annuity start date has an expected return multiple of 16.0, per Reg. §1.72-9, Table V).

If the annuitant outlives their life expectancy, every payment after the investment is fully recovered is 100% taxable (since basis is already used up). If they die before recovering the full investment, the unrecovered portion is deducted on their final return.

### Property Dispositions

Selling an asset can produce gain or loss. Consistent with the return-of-capital principle, basis comes back tax-free first. A loss occurs when proceeds fall short of basis — but a realized loss only reduces taxable income if it's actually deductible (not automatic).

**Basic gain/loss formula:**
```
    Sales proceeds
    Less: Selling expenses
    = Amount realized
    Less: Tax basis in property sold
    = Gain (Loss) on sale
```

How the gain is taxed (and whether a loss is deductible at all) depends heavily on whether the asset was used for business, investment, or personal purposes — personal-use losses generally aren't deductible.

### Other Sources of Gross Income

#### Income from Flow-Through Entities

Partnerships and S corporations don't pay entity-level tax on this income — it "flows through" to owners, who report their proportional share on their own returns (via Schedule K-1, tied to Forms 1065/1120-S). Each item keeps its original tax character (e.g., a qualified dividend earned by the entity is still a qualified dividend on the owner's return). Owners are taxed on their share of entity income whether or not cash is actually distributed. Cash distributions themselves are treated as a tax-free return of basis up to the owner's investment; anything beyond basis is taxable.

#### Alimony

To count as alimony for tax purposes, a payment must be: cash, made under a written separation/divorce agreement, not designated as something other than alimony, made while the spouses live apart, and must stop at the recipient's death. For agreements executed before 2019, qualifying alimony is taxable to the recipient and deductible (for AGI) by the payer — effectively shifting income between spouses.

Property divisions and court-ordered child support are *not* alimony, regardless of when the agreement was executed — the recipient excludes them from income and the payer gets no deduction.

#### Prizes, Awards, and Gambling Winnings

Generally fully taxable, with three narrow exceptions:

1. **Scientific/literary/charitable achievement awards** (e.g., Nobel Prize) — excluded only if the recipient didn't enter any contest to win it, isn't required to perform future services, and directs the payor to transfer the award straight to a government unit or qualified charity. (This "direct transfer" route is actually more tax-favorable than accepting the cash and separately donating it, since it functions like a for-AGI deduction rather than a from-AGI one.)
2. **Employee length-of-service or safety awards** — excluded up to $400/year in tangible (non-cash) property, as long as it isn't disguised compensation.
3. **Team USA Olympic/Paralympic medals and prize money** — excluded with no dollar cap, but the exclusion disappears entirely for a year in which the taxpayer's AGI (before the award) tops $1 million ($500,000 if MFS).

Gambling winnings are fully taxable; losses/expenses are deductible up to the amount of winnings — as an itemized deduction for casual gamblers, but as a for-AGI deduction for professional gamblers.

#### Social Security Benefits

Up to 85% of benefits can be taxable, depending on filing status and **modified AGI** (regular AGI excluding Social Security, plus tax-exempt interest and a few other add-backs). The calculation compares modified AGI plus half of benefits against tiered thresholds ($25,000/$34,000 for single filers, $32,000/$44,000 for MFJ; MFS filers generally get no threshold relief at all). Because these thresholds don't adjust for inflation, more retirees end up with 85% of benefits taxed as time goes on.

#### Imputed Income

Sometimes an indirect economic benefit counts as taxable income even without a direct cash transaction — this is **imputed income**. Two common triggers, both generally tied to non-arm's-length relationships (employer-employee, owner-entity, family members):

- **Bargain purchases** — buying something below market value. The tax character depends on the relationship: employee-from-employer is compensation, shareholder-from-corporation is a dividend, family-to-family is a gift. Employees do get a limited exclusion for employer discounts (goods up to the employer's gross profit margin, services up to 20%) — anything beyond that is taxable compensation.
- **Below-market loans** — the benefit is the gap between the market rate and what's actually charged (**imputed interest**). The law treats this as if the borrower paid the lender that difference, and the lender then "returned" it to the borrower — creating phantom interest income for the lender and interest expense for the borrower (deductibility depending on how the loan was used). The character of the "returned" amount again follows the relationship (compensation, dividend, or gift). This doesn't apply to small loans — generally $10,000 or less between the parties.

#### Discharge of Indebtedness

Forgiven debt is generally taxable income. Exception: insolvent taxpayers (liabilities exceeding assets, both before and after the discharge) don't recognize income from the forgiveness — though if the discharge pushes them into solvency, the amount that makes them solvent *is* taxable. There's also a specific exclusion for most student loan discharges occurring after 2020 and before 2026 (though not for discharges tied to services performed for the lending institution).

## Exclusion Provisions

Some realized income is permanently or temporarily kept out of the tax base — these are called **nonrecognition provisions**. Congress generally does this for two reasons: to subsidize/encourage certain behavior, or to avoid unfairness (like double taxation).

### Common Exclusions

#### Municipal Bond Interest

Interest on **municipal bonds** (state/local government debt) is excluded from federal tax — effectively a subsidy that lets these governments borrow at a lower rate. Flip side: interest on federal obligations (like Treasuries) is fully taxable federally, but exempt at the state/local level.

#### Gains on the Sale of Personal Residence

Homeowners who meet ownership and use tests can permanently exclude up to $250,000 ($500,000 MFJ) of gain on selling their principal residence. Gain above that is taxed as long-term capital gain. Both tests require 2+ years of qualifying ownership/use out of the 5 years before the sale (use doesn't need to be continuous). Generally limited to once every two years. For MFJ, only one spouse needs to meet the ownership test, but both need to meet the use test to get the full $500,000 — if either spouse already used their own $250,000 exclusion within the prior two years, the couple is capped at $250,000.

#### Fringe Benefits

Non-cash employer benefits (car use, gym memberships, moving expense payments, etc.) are generally taxable compensation — unless they qualify as a specifically designated "qualified" fringe benefit, in which case they're excluded. Employer retirement contributions similarly get deferred, not taxed immediately.

**Expense reimbursements**: if the employer requires documentation and only reimburses legitimate business expenses (an "accountable plan"), reimbursements are excluded and the employee can't separately deduct the expenses. Without proper documentation requirements, reimbursements become taxable compensation, with no offsetting deduction available.

### Education-Related Exclusions

#### Scholarships

Degree-seeking students can exclude scholarships (including Pell grants) used for tuition, fees, books, and required course supplies — amounts covering room/board are taxable. The exclusion requires no service obligation in exchange; "scholarships" that are really compensation are fully taxable. Tuition waivers for student employees (undergrad) or teaching/research assistants (grad) are not taxable.

Athletic scholarships qualify for the same exclusion as long as participation isn't strictly required, no substitute activity is mandated, and the scholarship doesn't get cancelled if the student can't play — subject to the same room/board carve-out. NIL compensation, separately, is always taxable.

#### Other Educational Subsidies

##### 529 Plans

Contributions grow and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualifying education costs — no annual cap for higher-education expenses (tuition, books, required equipment, reasonable room/board), but capped at $10,000/year/beneficiary for K-12 tuition. Non-qualifying withdrawals of earnings are taxable to the beneficiary plus a 10% penalty; withdrawn original contributions are treated as gifts. If a contributor (not the beneficiary) receives earnings back, that's taxable to them, plus the penalty.

##### Coverdell Accounts

Capped at $2,000/year per beneficiary in contributions; usable for K-12 and higher-ed expenses. The $2,000 limit phases out as AGI rises from $190,000–$220,000 (MFJ) or $95,000–$110,000 (others) — not inflation-indexed.

#### U.S. Series EE Bonds

These bonds are bought at a discount and pay out at redemption — the growth is normally taxable interest. But if redemption proceeds go toward qualifying higher-education expenses (or into a 529 plan) for the taxpayer, spouse, or dependent, the interest can be excluded. This exclusion phases out above certain modified AGI thresholds in the redemption year.

### Exclusions That Mitigate Double Taxation

#### Gifts and Inheritances

Gifts (transfers from a living person) and inheritances (transfers from an estate) are subject to federal *transfer* taxes (gift tax / estate tax), not income tax — so the recipient doesn't also owe income tax on them. This avoids taxing the same transfer twice under two different tax regimes.

#### Life Insurance Proceeds

Similar logic: proceeds may already be subject to estate tax at the decedent's level, so the beneficiary excludes the death benefit from income tax. If proceeds are paid out over time instead of as a lump sum, the interest portion of those installments is still taxable. The exclusion generally doesn't survive if the policy itself was sold to a third party for value — in that case, the purchaser can only exclude proceeds up to what they paid (purchase price + premiums), with the rest taxed as ordinary income.

If a policy is simply cashed out for its surrender value, any excess over premiums paid is ordinary income (losses aren't deductible). Terminally ill policyholders (certified death expected within 24 months) can receive accelerated death benefits tax-free; chronically ill policyholders can exclude proceeds used specifically for long-term care.

#### Foreign-Earned Income

U.S. citizens are taxed on worldwide income, which can create double taxation when a foreign country also taxes the same income. Relief options: exclude foreign-earned income up to an inflation-indexed cap ($126,500 in 2024 — note this doesn't cover pensions, annuities, U.S. government salary, or deferred comp), or instead deduct/credit foreign taxes paid. Taxpayers elect the exclusion via Form 2555; once revoked, they can't re-elect it for five years.

Qualifying for the exclusion requires a foreign tax home plus either full-year bona fide residence in the foreign country, or physical presence there for 330 days within any 12-month period. The exclusion is prorated for partial qualifying periods. There's also a related housing-cost exclusion for employer-paid housing exceeding 16% of the year's exclusion amount, capped at 14% of that same amount.

### Sickness and Injury-Related Exclusions

The general theme here: payments compensating for sickness/injury are treated like a return of "human capital," not income.

#### Workers' Compensation

Payments from a state workers' comp plan for a work-related injury are fully excluded — notably the opposite treatment from unemployment compensation, which is fully taxable.

#### Payments Associated with Personal Injury

All compensatory damages tied to a physical injury or sickness are excluded — including amounts for lost wages and for emotional distress *stemming from* the physical injury. Punitive damages, by contrast, are always taxable (they punish, they don't compensate). Emotional distress damages unconnected to any physical injury are also taxable, as is basically anything else not tied to physical injury/sickness.

#### Health Care Reimbursement

Reimbursements from health/accident insurance for medical expenses paid that year are excluded, regardless of who purchased the policy — subject to the tax benefit rule if the expense was deducted in a prior year.

#### Disability Insurance

More restrictive than the above. If you buy disability insurance yourself, premiums aren't deductible, but benefits are excluded. If an employer buys it, the employee often gets to choose: premiums treated as taxable compensation (benefits later excluded) or premiums treated as a tax-free fringe benefit (benefits later taxable). Either way, only one side of the transaction gets taxed, not both.

#### Deferral Provisions

Separate from permanent exclusions, deferral provisions push recognition to a later year rather than eliminating it — covering things like installment sales, like-kind exchanges, involuntary conversions, and contributions to traditional (non-Roth) retirement accounts. These get their own detailed treatment in later chapters.
